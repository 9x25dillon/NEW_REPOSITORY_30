# Session review — 2026-09-22

One session, one game, seven play reports. The work began as "add battle
mechanics, tools and allies" to the 2026-09-21 living-worlds pass and became
four rounds of build-measure-play-retune, each round steered by a report the
user pasted in.

This review separates what was built from what is still only hoped. It does not
claim the game is better; it claims what was measured, and names what was not.

## What shipped

| Round | Built | Answered which evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Riposte, king gambits (charge/shock/echo), companions as bodies in the water + R3 calls, mitochondrial repair, evolution cards, rack window | Aeon-6 report: 17 hits all volleys, 22 idle mitochondria, 100-cell rack, no tames |
| 2 | The falter | Four runs, 14 aeons, zero tames: one discharge crossed the whole bond window |
| 3 | Prime helpings, five new traits + uncapped REFINE, cards bought with cells, the lure, tender and leech | Aeon-29 report: every trait capped by the 19th birth, 140 idle cells, 31 helpings fed "for want of anything else to do" |
| 4 | Gambits' own 0.9 s wind, riposte retune, birth-screen nudge, diagnosis wording | Aeon-10 report: shock landing 40% of throws, nine births with nine cards and 280 cells left, a diagnosis that had gone stale |

Tests went 344 → 385. Chromium interaction checks went 14 → 36. Both
typechecks stayed clean throughout. `src/`, the instrument, was never touched.

## Key decisions, and what each one cost

| Decision | Why | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A catch needs a burst **into** the arm, within 60° | A sideways dodge was catching 26–40 arms/min by accident, killing a 2310 hp king in under a minute | The verb is harder to discover; the player still catches about one every two minutes |
| One catch per burst, then a 2.5 s recovery | Standing on a king caught its whole volley at once | Two rules to explain instead of none |
| Thrown arms worth ⅔ of mass per arm, doubled in round 4 | Tuned first against a bot's ceiling, which made the human floor worthless | The ceiling had to be cut by half (the recovery) to pay for it |
| Gambits on a 7 s clock, with a 0.9 s wind of their own | Cadence falls with mass, so "every third wind-up" meant 17 charges/min for a late king; a shared 0.55 s warning made shocks unavoidable | Gambits are rarer, so their variety arrives more slowly |
| A charge hits with a 36 µm core | A king fed twenty helpings is 142 µm in radius and hit on every use | The drawn lane is narrower than the body running down it |
| The falter: 2.5 s, floored at 1 hp, once per king | Bonding was unreachable: one discharge crossed the window | Kings cannot be one-shot from above the line any more |
| Prime helpings buy past the mass ceiling | The user's own idea, replacing my proposed gate: they overfed **on purpose** because nothing else was left to do | A number is rewarded for being itself — a game rule with no physical claim |
| REFINE, uncapped, as the deck's fallback | Every trait was capped by the 19th of 28 births | An unbounded stat, kept deliberately small (3%/rank) |
| Cards after the first cost cells, doubling | A rack of 140–300 cells had no sink | A second economy to learn; it went unused until a nudge was added |
| One `prey()` answers "what is the water coming for" | A lure some callers believed in would pull a hunter off you and walk a king onto you at once | Every new pursuer must remember to ask it |
| Tender and leech carry no strike | New pressure that is not more dodging | They are answered by holding, which is the game's oldest verb |

## Unresolved assumptions

1. **The riposte retune fixes the feel.** Doubling the value and halving the
   rate is arithmetic; "feels nerfed" was a feeling. Unverified.
2. **0.9 s is enough warning for a person.** Bots in an opened pool now take
   zero shock hits. A person with a controller is not a bot.
3. **Prime helpings change behaviour.** They may just be points collected while
   feeding the same way as before.
4. **The nudge makes card-buying visible.** One toast, once per birth.
5. **The lure will be used at all.** It has no prompt pushing anyone toward it,
   and the last report predates it.
6. **Companions have not trivialised hunters.** One landed strike in 32 minutes
   is a big change that has not been examined as a loss.
7. **Session length.** Runs are 24–50 minutes now. Nobody has said whether that
   is what is wanted.
8. **Physical controller.** R3 mid-fight, head-on bursts on a real stick, and
   whether the lure's held LB conflicts with lifting in practice.

## Three places I could have been more efficient

1. **I balanced the riposte against a ceiling and broke its floor.** I measured
   a bot catching 58 arms/min and tuned so that ceiling was sane. The report in
   front of me already said a person catches about one every two minutes; I
   never evaluated the number at the observed rate. It shipped weak, stayed
   weak for three runs, and the user had to tell me. Any tuning number should
   be checked at both ends: the rate a bot can reach and the rate the evidence
   says a person actually plays at.
2. **I trusted a measurement harness before validating it.** Three separate
   fixtures were wrong — a king that never crossed its bond line, a bot that
   walled itself into every charge, and a pool 21 times smaller than the one a
   real player fights in — and each produced a confident wrong conclusion
   ("charges hit on every use") that cost a round of analysis. The fix is one
   step: before believing a harness, make it reproduce a number the play report
   already contains.
3. **Process waste.** Eight full-suite runs (about two minutes each) where the
   focused files would have done until the end; two shells killed by a `pkill`
   pattern that matched its own command line; screenshots and rebuilds run
   serially in separate rounds instead of batched. None of it changed the
   result, all of it was wall-clock.

## Three places the collaboration could be more productive

These are about supplying context cheaply, not about writing perfect prompts.

1. **State the intent behind the behaviour, not just the behaviour.** The
   single most valuable sentence all session was "i over fed on purpose bc i
   ran out of things to do". Before it, I had designed a gate to stop you
   overfeeding — the exact opposite of what you wanted. It arrived in round 3;
   it existed in your head from round 1. A one-line "why I did that" attached
   to a report is worth more than the report.
2. **Annotate each run with what you ignored.** Reports say what happened, not
   what you meant to do. "Never used the lure", "forgot the riposte existed",
   "couldn't find the buy" separate a numbers problem from a visibility problem
   in one word — and those two have completely different fixes.
3. **Say the shape of the session up front.** "I have 40 minutes to play and
   want the late game less repetitive; don't touch the physics" would let me
   pick a size of change on purpose. Your mid-session steering was excellent
   ("don't gate the throne, reward primes") — that one sentence redirected a
   whole design. Earlier is cheaper.

A useful shape for the next report:

> Aeon [n], [time], died to [what]. I was trying to [goal]. I ignored [verb]
> because [reason]. This felt [good/bad]: [mechanic]. Report: [paste]

## Two words worth having

**Legibility** — how readable a system's state and intentions are to the person
playing it. A shock that lands 40% of the time is not too strong, it is
illegible: the information arrives too late to act on. Saying "this isn't hard,
it's illegible" points me at the telegraph rather than the numbers. Useful
prompt: *"The gambits are hard to read — work on legibility before difficulty."*

**Counterplay** — the specific answer a threat leaves open. Volleys had one
(stand in the gaps), so a long fight was attrition; the riposte added a second.
A threat with no counterplay is weather. Useful prompt: *"What's the
counterplay to a leech when my buildings are spread out?"*

And the phrase that explains this session's biggest mistake: **skill floor
versus skill ceiling**. I tuned the ceiling (what a perfect player could
extract) and forgot the floor (what a real player actually gets). Both numbers
matter, and only one of them was in front of me.
